Pillars

Training that changes the shot.

FPS aim is a visuomotor skill. Shooting science and motor-learning research keep finding the same sequence: get there, become still, commit the first bullet, then repeat with feedback. That is what this lab enforces.

01 · Move

Acquire the target.

Getting the sight onto the head is a reaching problem. In FPS mouse work, spatial control of the movement predicts whether you hit. Train the path in. Spray is not acquisition.

Warburton et al., 2023, PNAS Nexus

02 · Stop

Stillness is accuracy.

Among novice rifle shooters, postural balance and hold stability explained about a quarter of score. Elite shotgun work shows slower gun motion and a longer final gaze on hits. A walking shot is a moving platform. UNIT 12 only falls when you stop.

Mononen et al., 2007 · Causer et al., 2010

03 · Fire

One committed first bullet.

Quiet-eye research: the last stable fixation before the act is longer in elites and longer on successful shots. The lab scores the first true head at a stop — not the spray that follows a miss.

Causer et al., 2010, Med Sci Sports Exerc

04 · Repeat

Motor acuity, not more deathmatch.

In a large FPS practice dataset (7,174 players), hit percentage barely moved while hits-per-second — motor acuity — rose across days. About 65% of day-one learning carried overnight, then settled near 40%. Most of the next-day benefit showed up by roughly 30–50 minutes of focused practice. Short, honest reps beat grinding.

Listman et al., 2021, Front. Hum. Neurosci.

These papers describe the skills. They do not evaluate DTRT Lab. Fitts’ law (1954) is the classic speed–accuracy tradeoff behind acquisition. Quiet eye: Vickers. Rifle balance: Mononen, Konttinen, Viitasalo & Era, Scand J Med Sci Sports (n = 58). Shotgun gaze and gun motion: Causer, Bennett, Holmes, Janelle & Williams. FPS mouse kinematics: Warburton, Campagnoli, Mon-Williams, Mushtaq & Morehead. Longitudinal practice: Listman et al., N = 7,174.
